# Competitor snapshot

Use this when the user wants a quick, honest picture of a competitor for a client meeting, a pitch or a planning session. The user gives you notes, pasted text or figures. You do not look anything up.

## Before you write

Read `profile.md` for the user's tone and `context/entities.md` for the client the snapshot is for. If the user has not said which client or which competitor, ask first. A snapshot with no purpose is a list of facts nobody uses.

## What to produce

A single page with these parts, in this order. Leave out a part if the notes give you nothing for it.

1. **Who they are**: name, what they sell, who they sell to, rough size if the notes say. Two sentences.
2. **Positioning**: how they describe themselves, in their own words where the notes quote them, and what they seem to promise.
3. **What they do well**: three points at most, each backed by something in the notes.
4. **Where they are weak**: three points at most, each backed by something in the notes.
5. **Channels**: where they appear to advertise and publish, and how often, as far as the notes show.
6. **What it means for the client**: two or three plain sentences. What the client could do differently, and what they should not copy.

## Rules for honesty

- Separate what the notes say from what you infer. Mark inferences with "seems" or "probably".
- Never state a figure the notes do not contain. If a number is missing, write "not in the notes".
- Do not describe a competitor's internal strategy as fact. You can only see what is public.
- If the notes contradict each other, show both and say so.
- Keep the tone fair. A snapshot that only criticises is not useful to the client.

## Brand voice and tone

Northlight writes the way a good colleague talks: plainly, warmly and with the point first. We choose the short word over the long one, we explain jargon the first time it appears, and we never hide a problem in the middle of a paragraph.

We lead with the result and the numbers, then give the reason, then say what happens next. Every message to a client should leave them knowing three things: where things stand, what we are doing about it, and when they will hear from us again.

We do not use exclamation marks, filler praise or hype words such as game-changing, revolutionary or best-in-class. We use first names, British spelling and the active voice, and we sign off with the account manager's name rather than the agency.

## Worked example of the "What it means for the client" part

Suppose the notes say the competitor spends heavily on short video and has a lively community page. A weak version says: "They are strong on social, so we should do more social." A good version says: "They get most of their attention from short videos about product care. Acme does not have anything like it, and its customers ask the same questions. A small series of care videos would be a low-risk test. The community page is expensive to keep alive and is not worth copying."

## Questions to ask the user if the notes are thin

Which client is this for? What decision will it feed into? What do they already know about this competitor? Is there a competitor they fear more? Which of the points would the client most like to hear? Is there anything the client would be upset to see in writing?

## Formatting

Use short headings and plain sentences. Put the part called "What it means for the client" last and keep it to three sentences. Never use a table for prose. If the user asks for a slide version, give each part as one slide with a single sentence headline, and keep the same order.

## Checklist before you hand it over

Does every statement trace back to the notes? Are inferences marked? Is the tone fair? Does the last part say something the client can act on? Is it one page? Is the client named correctly as in `context/entities.md`?

## Longer guidance on reading competitor material

When the notes are pasted from a website, expect marketing language. Strip the adjectives and keep the claims: what they say they do, for whom, and at what price if given. When the notes are pasted from reviews, count how many people say the same thing before you treat it as a pattern: one review is a story, five reviews are a signal. When the notes are pasted from social media, pay attention to the posting rhythm and to which posts get replies rather than to the number of followers. When the notes are pasted from a job advertisement, read it as a hint about what the competitor is investing in, and say so with care, because job ads are often months old.

When the notes mention prices, record the price, what it includes and the date the note was taken, because prices change. When the notes mention a campaign, record its dates, its message and the channel, and say whether the notes show any result. When the notes mention a partnership, record who the partner is and what each side gets, as far as the notes say.

Keep a list of questions the notes could not answer. A short list of open questions is often the most useful part of a snapshot, because it tells the client what is worth finding out next and what is only speculation.

## When the user wants more than one page

Offer a second page with the evidence: the quotes, figures and dates the snapshot is based on, each with the place in the notes where it came from. Keep the first page unchanged. The second page is for people who want to check; the first page is for people who want to decide.

## What not to do

Do not make up a competitor's strengths to balance the page. Do not copy long passages from the notes; summarise them. Do not use the competitor's name in a way that could read as an accusation. Do not comment on people, only on what the company does.
